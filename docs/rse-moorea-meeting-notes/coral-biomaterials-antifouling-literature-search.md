# Coral Biomaterials and Antifouling Materials Literature Search

Search date: 2026-08-05

Purpose: identify published papers relevant to the Coral Gardeners wound-treatment idea: open wound versus concrete, Apoxie Sculpt epoxy, Scripps biomaterial, and related antifouling/fouling-control materials.

## Bottom line for the Moorea experiment

The literature supports a four-arm nursery-fragment wound trial, but the evidence is uneven across treatments.

1. **Concrete/cement has the strongest operational coral-restoration evidence.** It has been tested for outplant attachment, microfragment substrates, larval settlement devices, and alkaline substrate modification. It is cheap, scalable, and well studied, but its mass and chemistry can confound skeletal accretion measurements.

2. **Epoxy has field-use evidence as an attachment method, but little direct evidence as a wound-cover material.** Published restoration studies include generic epoxy resin comparisons. I did not find a coral-specific paper on Aves Apoxie Sculpt as a wound patch. Apoxie Sculpt should be treated as the practical epoxy benchmark, not as a well-validated biological treatment.

3. **The Scripps-linked biomaterials likely map to two different concepts that need to be separated.** SNAP-X and Brink are settlement-enhancing biomaterials for larvae/spat; Coral Guard/fouling-prevention coatings are antifouling/fouling-release approaches for protecting small corals from algal competition. Neither is automatically a safe direct-cover material for a fresh wound unless the formulation was built for tissue contact.

4. **Antifouling materials are promising, but direct tissue contact is the risk.** Non-biocidal fouling-release coatings and waxes have the best coral-restoration signal. Biocidal antifoulants, especially copper/TBT/Irgarol-class materials, are inappropriate near coral tissue. Encapsulated DCOIT reduced fouling in one settlement-plug study, but larval behavior and non-target toxicity remain concerns.

5. **For the wound experiment, the cleanest measurements are wound closure, fouling on wound/material margin, tissue recession, lesion color/condition, material retention, and scar skeletal infill.** Whole-fragment buoyant weight is useful only with material blanks because cement, epoxy, and biomaterials change mass independently of coral calcification.

## Candidate material classes for the experiment

**Open wound**

- Essential baseline for estimating the biological benefit of any cover.
- Expect high fouling risk, but possibly fastest recovery if covers irritate tissue or fail.

**Concrete/cement**

- Best-supported low-cost operational material.
- Needs standard recipe, cure/rinse protocol, and material blanks.
- Measure local fouling because cement may be a settlement/fouling substrate.

**Apoxie Sculpt epoxy**

- Operational benchmark from Adrian's current material use.
- No Apoxie-specific coral wound paper found.
- Need cure-state consistency, application-time measurement, retention scoring, and close inspection of tissue recession at the epoxy margin.

**Scripps biomaterial**

- First task is to identify the exact formulation: SNAP-X, Brink/Bacterial Reef Ink, Coral Guard/fouling-prevention coating, biopaste, or another Scripps/UCSD material.
- If it is a settlement cue hydrogel, the direct wound-cover rationale is weak unless it also improves tissue migration or resists fouling.
- If it is a fouling-release coating, test it as adjacent fouling control or a wound-margin material before applying directly over tissue.
- If it is active biopaste/sealant, include retention, cure, tissue irritation, and scar infill as primary endpoints.

## Design implications to add to the protocol

- Randomize treatments within parent colony/genotype and nursery location block.
- Include material blanks for concrete, epoxy, and biomaterial to quantify material mass change and fouling.
- Photograph with scale and color standard at T0, 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 9-12 months.
- Segment each image into live tissue, exposed skeleton, treatment material, algae/fouling, sediment, and dead tissue.
- Score material retention and margin irritation separately from wound closure.
- Record treatment handling time and failure/reapplication events.
- Treat "anti-fouling" as a measurable mechanism: lower fouling should mediate faster closure or better condition, not just be a treatment label.
