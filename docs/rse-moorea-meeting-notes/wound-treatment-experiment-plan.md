## Working objective

Coral Gardeners regularly creates coral fragments and, in doing so, creates exposed skeletal surfaces that can become sites of fouling, tissue recession, disease, or slow recovery. This experiment asks whether covering those wounds improves recovery enough to justify the added material cost and handling time.

The practical decision is not simply which material produces the cleanest wound. The useful treatment is the one that improves healing, limits fouling, does not harm live tissue, stays in place long enough to matter, and can be applied efficiently by the nursery team.

## Core question

Do wound covers improve tissue closure and skeletal recovery of nursery fragments enough to justify their cost, handling time, and failure risk?

## Treatment comparison

The experiment should compare four wound treatments on nursery fragments:

1. **Open wound control**

   Exposed skeleton is left uncovered. This treatment estimates natural wound closure and natural fouling rates under nursery conditions.

2. **Concrete patch**

   Concrete tests whether a cheap mineral cover can reduce fouling and support recovery. The key risks are poor adhesion, surface roughness, and sediment or algal accumulation.

3. **Apoxie Sculpt epoxy**

   Apoxie Sculpt tests the practical epoxy material currently used by Adrian. The key risks are handling time, cure state, adhesion, tissue irritation at the margin, and whether the epoxy itself becomes a fouling surface.

4. **Scripps / Hybrid Reefs biomaterial candidate**

   Working assumption after fuzzy literature/web search: this likely refers to Daniel Wangpraseurt's Scripps/UCSD Hybrid Reefs materials. For a nursery-fragment wound or reskinning experiment, the best candidate is probably **CoralGuard**, an antifouling plug/tile/coating system designed to reduce algal overgrowth and promote coral tissue growth/fusion. Nearby Scripps materials such as SNAP-X and Brink are larval-settlement coatings, so they are less directly relevant unless Hannah meant a recruitment experiment rather than a wound-cover experiment.

   CoralGuard should be included as a **candidate mechanism**, not as the presumed best treatment. It is attractive because the proposed mechanism, reduced algal fouling and improved tissue growth across a substrate, matches one plausible wound-failure pathway. But it may be the wrong tool if the key problem is acute tissue damage at a fresh cut face, if the material is designed for plugs/tiles rather than exposed skeleton, if it requires pre-fabricated geometry or curing conditions that do not work in the nursery, or if it cannot be applied without contacting live tissue. The proposal should therefore frame this treatment as a test of whether an antifouling/reskinning substrate can outperform simpler physical covers, not as a proven wound-healing material.

An optional fifth group would be an **unwounded handling reference**: fragments are handled, photographed, and returned to the nursery without wounding. This does not test wound treatment, but it helps separate wound effects from handling and nursery-position effects.

## Hypotheses and predictions

| Hypothesis | Mechanism | Prediction | Result that would argue against it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barrier Protection Hypothesis | Any physical cover protects exposed skeleton from fouling and sediment, allowing tissue to advance across the wound. | Concrete, epoxy, and biomaterial all reduce fouling and increase wound closure relative to open wounds. Material differences are small. | Covered wounds foul as much as open wounds, or do not close faster. |
| Antifouling Reskinning Hypothesis | A CoralGuard-like material reduces algal/sediment fouling and creates a better surface for tissue migration than inert covers. | The Scripps/Hybrid Reefs candidate has faster closure, lower fouling, stronger scar infill, or lower tissue recession than concrete and epoxy. | The candidate performs no better than concrete or epoxy, increases tissue irritation, fails to adhere, or only works when used as a plug/tile rather than as a wound cover. |
| Fouling Substrate Hypothesis | Added materials create rough or persistent surfaces that attract algae, sediment, or invertebrates. | Covered wounds, especially rough or poorly retained materials, foul more than open wounds and show slower closure. | Covers reduce fouling and do not increase recession. |
| Operational Drag Hypothesis | A treatment can work biologically but still fail because it is slow, expensive, or does not stay attached. | One or more covered treatments improves healing but has poor cost-adjusted performance. | The best biological treatment is also cheap, fast, and durable. |
| No-Effect Hypothesis | Nursery fragments heal similarly regardless of cover material. | Closure, fouling, accretion, survival, and condition do not differ meaningfully among treatments. | Any treatment produces a clear biological or cost-adjusted advantage. |

## Why CoralGuard may not be the right treatment

CoralGuard belongs in the proposal because it is a plausible high-upside material, but the match to a wound-cover experiment is not guaranteed. Its published and public-facing use case is closer to antifouling plugs, tiles, microfragment growth, and fragment fusion than to sealing fresh cut faces. That distinction matters: a material can be excellent for reducing algal competition around a settled fragment and still be a poor direct wound treatment if it irritates live tissue, fails on irregular branch surfaces, traps sediment at the wound margin, or adds too much application time.

The proposal should therefore include a short **formulation check** before committing it to the full experiment:

1. Confirm whether Hannah meant CoralGuard, SNAP-X, Brink, or another Scripps/Hybrid Reefs material.
2. Identify the intended use case: wound cover, wound-margin coating, plug/tile substrate, larval-settlement cue, or heat-stress mitigation.
3. Test application on a small number of non-experimental fragments or material blanks.
4. Keep CoralGuard in the main experiment only if it can be applied reproducibly, remains attached through the early fouling window, and does not cause obvious tissue recession.

If CoralGuard is really a plug/tile or fusion material, the better experiment may be separate from the wound-cover trial: compare CoralGuard-style nursery substrates against current plugs/tiles for fouling, tissue expansion, fragment fusion, and cost per outplant-ready fragment.

## Experimental unit

The experimental unit is the individual nursery fragment. Treatments should be assigned to fragments from known parent colonies or genotypes, with treatment assignment balanced within parent colony wherever possible.

The main design principle is to avoid confounding treatment with genotype. If fragments from one parent colony all receive epoxy and another parent colony all receive concrete, treatment effects cannot be separated from genotype effects. The best design assigns fragments from each parent colony across all four treatments.

## Blocking and randomization

Treatments should be randomized within blocks defined by:

- species or genus;
- parent colony or genotype;
- initial fragment size class;
- nursery structure type, such as rope or wire;
- nursery position or neighborhood;
- collection/nursery cohort.

The most defensible design is:

1. Select healthy nursery fragments from multiple parent colonies or genotypes.
2. Group fragments into similar size classes.
3. Within each parent colony or genotype, randomly assign replicate fragments to open wound, concrete, Apoxie Sculpt, and the Scripps/Hybrid Reefs candidate if the formulation check supports using it as a wound treatment.
4. Return fragments to randomized nursery positions so that one treatment is not concentrated in a better or worse microenvironment.
5. Record the parent colony/genotype, fragment size, nursery structure, and position for every fragment.

## Wound creation

The wound should be standardized in size, location, and geometry. The wound must be large enough to measure from photographs but small enough that mortality is not the expected outcome.

Two wound geometries are worth considering:

- **Cut-face wound:** a clipped branch end or standardized cut surface. This best mimics nursery fragmentation and outplant preparation.
- **Side-scar wound:** a standardized scrape or drill mark on the side of the fragment. This better mimics source-colony sampling wounds.

For a nursery-fragment experiment, the cut-face wound is probably the most operationally relevant. If Hannah's main interest is healing of donor colony scars, the side-scar wound is the better biological match. The first decision before field setup is which wound geometry matches the question Coral Gardeners most wants answered.

At T0, record:

- wound type;
- wound area from a standardized photograph;
- fragment length, width, and height;
- parent colony/genotype;
- treatment assigned;
- treatment footprint area;
- whether treatment overlaps live tissue;
- application time per fragment;
- any immediate tissue damage or handling problems.

## Measurements

### Primary biological responses

- **Wound closure:** percent exposed skeleton remaining, tissue-margin advance, and time to closure.
- **Fouling:** percent of the wound or treatment footprint covered by algae, sediment, cyanobacteria, sponges, worms, bivalves, or other invertebrates.
- **Skeletal accretion:** visible scar infill, new skeletal relief, or calcification proxy around the wound.
- **Coral condition:** health score, bleaching score, disease signs, tissue recession beyond the wound, and alive/dead status.

### Primary operational responses

- **Treatment retention:** present/absent, percent retained, cracking, peeling, displacement, or overgrowth.
- **Handling cost:** preparation time, underwater application time, material cost, and reapplication/failure rate.

### Useful secondary covariates

- Initial fragment size and volume proxy.
- Nursery structure type: rope, wire, or other.
- Nursery position and local fouling pressure.
- Neighbor identity, genotype, or distance if the nursery layout can be reconstructed.
- Fish, crab, snail, or other associates.
- Sediment load in the local nursery position.

## Measuring fouling

Fouling should be a primary endpoint because it is one of the main mechanisms by which an exposed wound or added material could fail.

A simple photo-based scoring workflow would work:

1. Take a standardized close-up photo of each wound at every time point.
2. Delineate the original wound or treatment footprint.
3. Estimate percent cover by fouling category.
4. Record whether fouling contacts live tissue.
5. Record whether fouling is associated with tissue recession.

Recommended fouling categories:

- turf algae;
- macroalgae;
- cyanobacteria or microbial film;
- sediment;
- sponge;
- worm tube;
- bivalve or other sessile invertebrate;
- unknown/other.

## Measuring skeletal accretion

Skeletal accretion is important, but the measurement needs care because concrete and epoxy add non-coral mass. Raw buoyant weight alone would confound coral growth with material mass, water absorption, and treatment loss.

Better options, from simplest to most involved:

1. **Photo-derived scar-infill score**

   Score visible skeletal infill or raised skeletal growth at the wound margin from standardized macro photos. This is likely the most practical first-pass measurement.

2. **Photo-derived wound closure plus fragment growth**

   Combine percent wound closure with length, width, height, or volume change. This gives both local wound recovery and whole-fragment growth.

3. **Photogrammetry or macro-depth reconstruction**

   If imagery supports it, estimate vertical relief or infill volume across the wound surface.

4. **Endpoint inspection**

   Sacrifice or remove a small subset at the endpoint, remove treatment material if possible, and inspect the scar surface for skeletal infill.

5. **Fluorescent skeletal staining**

   Use calcein or alizarin staining if feasible and permitted, then evaluate new skeletal growth at the wound margin.

6. **Buoyant weight with material blanks**

   If buoyant weight is used, deploy material blanks for concrete, epoxy, and biomaterial in the nursery to estimate material mass change. Treat buoyant weight as whole-fragment growth, not clean wound accretion.

## Sampling schedule

The schedule should capture early material failure and fouling, then longer-term healing:

| Time point | Main purpose |
|---|---|
| T0 | Photograph wound, record treatment, fragment size, wound area, application time, and immediate problems. |
| 2 weeks | Detect early treatment loss, fouling, sediment accumulation, and tissue-margin response. |
| 1 month | Estimate early wound closure and fouling category/extent. |
| 3 months | Main biological comparison for healing, survival, and condition. |
| 6 months | Main operational comparison for complete closure, scar infill, treatment persistence, and cost-adjusted value. |
| 9-12 months | Optional follow-up if healing is slow or if Coral Gardeners wants alignment with existing wound-monitoring intervals. |

The 2-week visit is important. If a material fails or fouls quickly, waiting until 3 months could miss the mechanism.

## Sample-size target

The experiment should prioritize balanced parent-colony/genotype replication over maximum sample size.

Recommended design if nursery space and fragments allow:

- 4 wound treatments.
- 8-12 parent colonies or genotypes.
- 3-5 fragments per treatment per parent colony/genotype.
- 96-240 wounded fragments total.
- Optional unwounded handling references.

Lean design:

- 4 wound treatments.
- 6 parent colonies or genotypes.
- 3 fragments per treatment per parent colony/genotype.
- 72 wounded fragments total.

If space is tight, keep all four treatments and reduce replicate fragments per parent colony. Do not assign treatments by parent colony, because that would make genotype and treatment inseparable.

## Analysis plan

The main analysis should estimate treatment effects while accounting for repeated measurements and parent-colony/genotype differences.

Primary response models:

- wound closure through time;
- fouling probability and percent fouled area;
- skeletal accretion or scar-infill score;
- treatment retention/failure;
- health, bleaching, disease, and survival;
- cost per successfully healed fragment.

Core predictors:

- treatment;
- time;
- treatment x time;
- initial wound area;
- initial fragment size;
- species/genus;
- parent colony or genotype;
- nursery structure;
- nursery position or neighborhood.

Key contrasts:

- any cover versus open wound;
- concrete versus Apoxie Sculpt;
- Scripps/Hybrid Reefs candidate versus simple physical covers;
- best biological treatment versus best cost-adjusted treatment.

The analysis should report effect sizes and uncertainty, not just whether a treatment is statistically different. For Coral Gardeners, the useful result is the expected improvement in healing per unit cost and effort.

## Decision criteria

A treatment should move forward only if it clears both biological and operational thresholds.

Biological thresholds:

- faster wound closure than open wounds by 3 or 6 months;
- lower fouling or delayed fouling;
- evidence of skeletal infill or accretion at the wound surface;
- no increase in tissue recession, disease, bleaching, or mortality.

Operational thresholds:

- treatment remains attached through the relevant healing period;
- application is fast enough for field use;
- material cost is acceptable at scale;
- failure/reapplication rates are low;
- treatment does not create a later removal or fouling problem.

Additional threshold for CoralGuard or any Scripps/Hybrid Reefs material:

- the material must match the biological problem being tested. If its mechanism is substrate conditioning or larval settlement rather than wound closure, it should be moved into a substrate/fusion experiment instead of treated as a wound-cover arm.

## Practical next steps

1. Confirm which coral taxa, parent colonies, and nursery structures are available for a balanced pilot.
2. Choose the wound geometry: cut-face wound for nursery-fragment relevance, or side-scar wound for source-colony wound relevance.
3. Confirm with Hannah whether the Scripps material is Wangpraseurt/Hybrid Reefs **CoralGuard**, SNAP-X, Brink, or another formulation; then decide whether it belongs in the wound-cover trial or in a separate substrate/fusion trial.
4. Standardize the concrete and Apoxie Sculpt preparation protocols before field deployment.
5. Create a field datasheet that includes fragment ID, parent colony/genotype, treatment, wound area, treatment footprint, application time, health, bleaching, fouling, retention, and photo ID.
6. Pilot the application procedure on a small number of non-experimental fragments to make sure the materials can be applied consistently.
7. Launch the randomized blocked experiment once treatment application is reproducible.

## Bottom line

The strongest design is a randomized, blocked nursery-fragment trial that compares open wounds against concrete, Apoxie Sculpt, and a Scripps/Hybrid Reefs candidate only if the candidate is appropriate for direct wound treatment. The experiment should measure fouling and skeletal accretion directly, not just survival or health, and it should treat cost and handling time as core outcomes. The decision is whether any wound cover produces enough biological gain to be worth using operationally; if CoralGuard is mainly a substrate or fusion technology, it should become a separate nursery-substrate experiment rather than a wound-treatment arm.
